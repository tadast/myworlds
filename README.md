# My Worlds

Type a name or a word. Get a small low-poly planet. The same word always gives the same world.

Static site: HTML, CSS, and JavaScript modules. No build step, no backend, no Node.

## Run locally

Serve the directory over HTTP. Web Workers do not run from `file://`.

```sh
ruby -rwebrick -e 's=WEBrick::HTTPServer.new(:Port => 5555, :DocumentRoot => Dir.pwd).start'
```

Open <http://localhost:5555/>. Add `#YourName` to the URL to open a specific world.

## Deploy to GitHub Pages

Push the repository and enable Pages for the branch root. `.nojekyll` keeps Jekyll away from `vendor/`.

## Icons and the share image

`icon.svg` draws the planet mark. `favicon.ico` (16, 32, 48) and `assets/apple-touch-icon.png` (180) come from it:

```sh
rsvg-convert -w 16 -h 16 icon.svg -o /tmp/i16.png
rsvg-convert -w 32 -h 32 icon.svg -o /tmp/i32.png
rsvg-convert -w 48 -h 48 icon.svg -o /tmp/i48.png
magick /tmp/i16.png /tmp/i32.png /tmp/i48.png favicon.ico
rsvg-convert -w 180 -h 180 icon.svg | magick png:- -background '#070a16' -alpha remove assets/apple-touch-icon.png
```

`assets/share.png` (1200x630) is the Open Graph card. `tools/share-image.html` composes it: the real app in an iframe for the planet, the title over it. Serve the directory, open the page at 1200x630, wait for the world, and save the frame. The seed is `Auralis`, so the planet is always the same one.

## How it works

- `worker.js` turns the seed into a hash, a PRNG, and seeded simplex noise. It builds an icosphere, raises terrain, paints biomes per face, places flora and fauna, and condenses clouds. It runs off the main thread and reports progress.
- `app.js` renders the result with three.js (flat-shaded vertex colours, instanced flora, fauna and clouds, atmosphere shaders, rings, moons). It saves each world's seed, type, and a thumbnail to `localStorage`.
- `site.js` picks the landing site for the probe: a ray from the pointer onto the surface, a snap to the cell grid, a pull to a creature that lives in the cell, a square marker on the ground, and the URL codec for the site and the camera. A cell is 0.01 units of arc across, about 74 km on a 7,352 km planet, and it is the same size on the screen for every planet. The globe draws its surface from an icosphere at detail 100, which puts about 0.011 units between two vertices, so a smaller square would sit inside one facet and the coast the reader aims at would not be where the field puts it. The square is the true footprint of the patch, so what it holds is what the ground shows, and it takes the ground height at each of its four corners so it follows the relief.
- `ground.js` holds the ground the probe lands on. The globe is a miniature at 1 unit for the planet radius. The ground is true scale: 1 unit is 1 metre. It keeps a second scene, a second camera, and second controls, and it draws with the same renderer. A sky dome of 5,000 m takes the atmosphere colour, and fog in the same colour runs from 450 m to 750 m at the ground, so the horizon fades and the patch shows no hard edge. The fog opens with the height of the camera, out to 1,325 m at the ceiling of 500 m, so the reader looks over the patch and not down on it. The ground runs on to 3,150 m from the site, well past the fog, so the reader never sees where it ends.
- The ground patch is the cell the reader picked, drawn into a box 1,500 units square. The cell is far wider than the box, so the ground holds an artificial scale: one unit is tens of metres across, and the vertical scale is set so every patch shows about 120 units of large-scale relief. A cell flatter than that keeps true height. Plants and animals keep their lore size in units, so they read as normal against the ground and they are no longer the metres the lore text says. The trade is deliberate: a patch of 1,500 true metres is under 2 px on the globe at the closest zoom, so the reader could not aim at a coast, and a coast almost never crossed a window that narrow. The worker builds it while the probe falls, and the dive holds the screen until the reply lands. A height grid with a vertex every 2 units, or every 4 on a low tier, carries the globe field read on a grid of 65 by 65 across the cell and interpolated, and three octaves of new noise: hills, knolls, and rock. The globe field used to come in as one linear tilt, because over 1,500 m the field is a plane; a cell bends inside it and a coast can cross it, so the field is now read per cell. The temperature and the moisture bend with it, so one patch can hold a beach, a meadow, and a forest, and three species instead of one. The colours follow the biome rules of the globe, so the ground reads in the colours the reader saw from orbit, with rock on the steep slopes and a lightness jitter per vertex. `ground.js` turns the grid into indexed flat-shaded meshes, and adds a rim: a second grid at 50 units per cell that carries the same globe field and the same hills out to 3,150 units from the site. The rim used to hold the height of the patch edge and drag it outward, which combed the edge into streaks; now the relief and the coast run on across the join. One mesh and one material draw the whole rim, and four dense strips tie it to the patch so the join holds no crack. The patch fades its knolls and its rock out over its last 100 units, because a rim cell cannot carry a wave that short, and it thins its plants away over its last 300 units, because the rim carries none. The camera stays 2 m above the terrain and at most 500 m above the site, and its target stays inside the fog start. Near the ground the view also turns over the horizon, up to the angle that holds the horizon on the bottom edge of the frame, so the reader can look up at the sky and at what flies in it. The eye keeps its floor there and the target rises instead, so the pivot of an up-view stands in the sky and the reader turns the head without walking. The probe arrives 450 m up and 884 m south of the site, so the reader sees the patch first and then zooms in. The ceiling and the tilt come from the fog: the fog opens 1.15 m for each metre of height, but the far edge of the box moves away only by the horizontal distance of the camera from the site, so the detail of the patch stops inside the fog and reads as a rectangle whenever the view looks down more steeply than 48 degrees.
- `ground-flora.js` grows the plants on that ground, and `flora-geometry.js` holds the shape of one plant for both the globe and the ground. The worker places up to 20,000 plants over the patch, or 6,000 on a low tier. One cell of 6 units takes at most one plant, the plant jitters inside its cell, and a plant closer than 3 units to a neighbour is dropped. A cell below sea level, on a beach, or on a slope steeper than 0.9 takes nothing, except that rock and crystal may stand on a slope.
- The patch holds sixteen kinds of plant. The globe grows the first seven: tree, pine, cactus, crystal, mushroom, boulder, and palm. The ground adds nine alien kinds: a tower mushroom that stands over any tree, a spindle of bare whips with a bud at each tip, a sac that breathes in and out, a crystal that pushes out of the ground, a tuft of grass, a colossus, a ribbed fan, a stalk of hung pods, and a stack of balanced plates. A kind carries a style: how much it glows, how far the wind bends it, how far it breathes, how far out it must stay a mesh, and how far one plant of that kind may lean and spread against the next.
- Every world holds a flora signature, so two planets never grow the same plants. The signature is a hash of the seed. It moves seven numbers before a kind lays out its shape: how thick a stalk stands, how wide a crown spreads, how flat a cap sits, how many parts a plant of many parts carries, how far the parts lean out, whether the plant carries a node that glows, and how far the derived hues turn from the palette. So the tower mushroom of one world is a slim spire and the tower mushroom of the next is a squat parasol, and a spindle carries four whips here and nine there. The signature also picks the kinds: a world borrows one to three from the whole alien set and drops up to two its type usually grows, so two temperate worlds hold different lists. The globe reads the same signature, so the plants a reader sees from orbit are the plants the ground shows.
- Four noise fields shape a patch, so the ground is not one even carpet. The community field says which kinds live where: a patch rolls four to seven communities, each with a lead kind of its own, a companion, a density, and a vigour, and two fields at two wavelengths lay them over the box as a patchwork. The grove field of 46 units knots them into thickets. The bare field of 195 units opens ground that holds nothing. The vigour field of 165 units says how big they grow, so one part of the box stands stunted and another stands tall. A size comes off a curve that bunches at the small end, with a rare giant, and it stops at 1.9 of the top of the range of its kind, so only a colossus reaches the size of a colossus.
- Two passes run after the scan and both keep their plants past the cap, because the cap must not drop a landmark. The arrangements stand seven to nineteen plants of one kind in a ring, an arc, a row, or a spiral, so the reader asks who made them. The colossus pass drops one to three bodies of 70 to 150 units, each with a court of smaller plants, spaced at least 340 units apart.
- Every plant carries its own lean, its own width against its height, and its own tint, all from the hash of its index. The tint rides on `instanceColor`, which the LOD walk copies beside the matrix, and the card quad takes a white colour attribute so the same tint multiplies the baked picture. The wind and the breath run in the vertex shader before the instance matrix, so both take the lean, the width, and the size of the instance for free.
- The ground carries grass between the plants. Grass is not one of the plants: a tuft is about one unit wide, so it only reads within about 80 units, and a field that wide over the whole box holds far more tufts than the cap. The worker writes a cover mask instead, one byte per node of a grid at twice the terrain step, from the biome, the slope, the temperature, the moisture, and the bare field. `GrassField` grows a lattice of up to about 8,800 tufts around the camera from it, 2,000 on a low tier. The lattice lives in world space and a tuft takes its place from the hash of its cell, so a tuft never moves under the reader: the field only gains cells at one edge and loses them at the other, and it rebuilds in about 0.8 ms when the camera has moved 7 units. The tufts shrink to nothing over the last 22 units, so no ring shows, and the whole field fades out in the shader as the reader climbs past 60 units. Each tuft takes the hue of the terrain under it, so the grass and the ground read as one surface.
- A plant is a real size in units: a tree 5 to 16, a pine 7 to 22, a palm 6 to 14, a cactus 2 to 6, a crystal 1.5 to 8, a mushroom 1 to 4, a boulder 0.8 to 5, a tower mushroom 19 to 42, a spindle 4 to 26, a sac 1.5 to 7, a growing crystal 2 to 12, a fan 3 to 10, a pod 2 to 9, a stack 3 to 15, and a colossus 70 to 150.
- The plants draw at two levels of detail. A plant within the LOD distance of the camera is the full mesh. That distance starts at 150 m and the frame time moves it; see the adaptive LOD below. The style of a kind multiplies it, because a card cannot carry a body that fills the screen: a colossus takes 14 and stays a mesh out to the fog, a tower mushroom takes 3, and a tuft of grass takes 0.3. A plant beyond it is a card: one vertical quad that carries a picture of the same mesh, baked once per kind at patch load into a target of 96 by 96 pixels, 160 for the tower, 192 for the colossus, and 48 for a tuft of grass, with the colours and the sun of the site.
- The card also holds a floor of its own, and the knob cannot pull a plant inside it. A plant `s` units tall and `d` units away covers `s * focal / d` pixels, where `focal` is the height of the view over twice the tangent of half the field of view. Past `s * focal / card` the picture is no longer magnified and the swap is invisible; nearer than that the reader sees a blown-up picture and calls the plant flat. The knob alone used to decide, and on a 30 Hz display it fell to its floor of 40 m and put 8,018 of 8,126 plants on cards, some of them ten metres away. The floor is per plant, not per kind, because one kind holds sizes from 5 units to 40. It costs one multiply and one compare in the walk, which still runs in 0.2 ms for 8,000 plants. The card turns toward the camera around the y axis in the vertex shader, and it dims by the angle between the eye and the sun, so a card and the mesh beside it hold one brightness. The card takes an alpha test and no blending, so it writes depth and sorts with the terrain. Every frame one walk reads the distance of every plant and writes it to the near mesh or to the card mesh, with a band of 10% around that distance so a plant at the boundary cannot flicker. The matrices are built once at load and the walk only copies them, so it costs about 0.4 ms for 20,000 plants. A forest site at 30 m over the ground draws in about 4.4 ms of card time, terrain included.
- `ground-fauna.js` puts the animals on that ground, at the size their lore states and in the groups their sociality gene asks for. The worker rolls 10 to 30 groups per patch, up to 300 animals, from the species whose niche lies within 300 m of the site, and the species the site was pulled to is always there. One mover carries the anchor of each group over a leash of 60 to 200 m at 0.5 to 6 m/s, and the members hold their place in the formation, turn to face the way they travel, and stand on the terrain, tilted to its slope. A flyer holds 12 to 40 m above the ground under it, so it clears a hill, and it rises and falls 1.4 m on a slow clock of its own. A flyer also gets three rules the walkers do not need, because the sky holds nothing to measure it against: it keeps its full mesh 2.5 times as far out, it never draws narrower than 11 px, and it lays a soft shadow on the ground under it at the slant of the sun. The shadow is the cue that makes the reader look up. A serpent and a plough follow the path of the anchor with a lag, so the chain reads. The whole herd stops and starts together, because every speed in the group comes from one anchor, but each animal keeps its own speed inside the formation, and its legs read that. No member travels faster than 1.35 of its cruise speed, so a wide formation swinging round a turn stretches instead of dragging its outer animals sideways. A tap on an animal marks it and glides the view to it: a ring in the accent of the palette lies on the ground under it and follows it, and a floating button at the foot of the screen offers its inspector card. A tap on a flyer over the reader turns the view up to it instead of walking to it, because the target rides the ground and cannot reach it. The test for the animal under the pointer takes the nearest one to the camera whose body holds the point, and it drops an animal that stands behind the hill the ray meets, so a tap never carries the reader off to an animal somewhere else. On a site with 299 animals the steering costs about 0.10 ms of the frame.
- The animals draw at two levels of detail, as the plants do. An animal within the same LOD distance is the full creature of 100 to 980 triangles. An animal beyond it is a coarse creature of 16 to 70 triangles, built from the same genome by the same builder: a ball becomes an octahedron, a tube becomes a three-sided open cylinder, a leg becomes one tapered bone with no knee, a chain body becomes one tapered tube, and the head furniture, the eyes, and every extra but the sail and the plates go. Each octahedron reaches exactly as far as the ball it replaces, and both levels take one scale from the full build, so a swap changes neither the size nor the place of an animal. The same shader animates both, with the leg swing at half the amplitude so a leg of a few pixels does not shimmer. The walk that places the animals also sorts them into the two meshes, with a band of 10% around that distance so an animal at the boundary cannot flicker, and the far mesh casts no shadow. Over a forest site with 299 animals this takes 200,000 triangles out of the frame and about 1 ms off the graphics card, and the whole ground draws in about 5.6 ms.
- `ground-sea.js` puts water on that ground when the world owns an ocean and the patch dips below the water line. The sea is one plane at sea level in the ocean colour and the ocean opacity of the world, so the sea bed reads through it. The bed carries the shore: a sand strip over the first 1.5 m of land, and a colour that darkens with the depth over the first 12 m of water. Two crossed sine sets in the vertex shader lift the plane by 0.12 m, one 2 m long with a period of 6 s and one 5 m long with a period of 9 s, so the water shows facets a few metres across that move at a walking pace and no swell crosses the patch. A lava sea glows and moves less; an ice sea is still and opaque. The grid holds 1 m cells within 200 m of the camera target and follows it, the flat water past that takes two coarse rings, and a block that stands under land is not drawn. Over open sea the fog takes 30% of the ocean colour and the camera floor is 2 m above the water. The sea costs about 1.4 ms of the frame.
- `ground-sky.js` draws the sky over that ground, so the sky says which world you stand on. The app turns the sun, the moons, and the ring of the globe into the frame of the site, because only the app knows how far the planet has turned. The dome carries a gradient: the atmosphere colour at the horizon, a 30% darker blend at the zenith, a warm tint within 20 degrees of the sun, and a disc at the sun. A sun under the horizon rises to 8 degrees, so the ground stays lit, and the sky goes to the dark end of the palette. Each moon runs its own orbit and sets at the horizon. A ringed world gets the ring as a band: at the ring plane it is a line through the zenith, and it opens into a wide arc at a higher latitude. Six to twelve flat clouds drift near the ceiling. The fog takes the horizon colour of the dome, so the far terrain and the sky end at one colour and the horizon holds no seam. The sky costs about 0.4 ms of the frame.
- `perf.js` holds the clock of the frame. It keeps two rolling averages over the last 30 frames: the interval between two frames, which is the frame time the reader sees, and the work the app does inside one frame. It reads the refresh rate of the display from the first 60 frames, and the target is 1,000 ms divided by that rate, at 60 Hz at most. A frame during the dive, a frame in the first second after load, and a frame over 100 ms do not count, because none of the three says anything about the scene.
- One knob keeps that target: the LOD distance of the ground. `Ground.update()` reads the clock every 500 ms. Over the target by 10% it multiplies the distance by 0.85. With the display at its refresh and the app under 70% of the frame it multiplies by 1.1, and it stays between 40 m and the ceiling of the device tier, 400 m or 250 m. The two steps read two numbers, because the display holds the interval at the refresh and the interval can therefore never show the room that is left. A step down also stops a step up for three seconds, so the knob cannot ring around the value where the machine sits exactly at the refresh. The plants and the animals both read the knob, and nothing else follows it: the shadows and the pixel ratio stay device-tier decisions. The settled distance goes to `localStorage` per tier, so the next landing starts near the right value. The sun casts only while the camera is lower than the knob over the ground, and that gate takes a band from 1.05 to 1.35 of the distance and holds each state for 1.5 s, so a knob that moves cannot switch the shadow on and off.
- Add `?perf` to the address for a small overlay, for example `http://localhost:5555/?perf#Auralis`. It shows the frame time, the work, the target and the refresh rate, the LOD distance, the near and far counts of the plants, the tuft count and the build time of the grass, the near and far counts of the animals, the shadow, the height of the camera, and the draw calls and triangles of the last frame. It reads twice a second, and it works in orbit as well, with the numbers of the globe. It stands at the top right, because the sidebar owns the left of a wide screen and the foot of a narrow one. Without the flag the page builds no overlay and does no work for it.
- One object in `app.js` holds the whole device tier. A coarse pointer, a screen under 600 px on its short side, or four processor cores or fewer put the reader on the low tier. `Q` is the globe and `Q.ground` is the probe, and every part reads the tier from there. A low device gets a probe with a height grid every 4 m instead of 2 m, 6,000 plants instead of 20,000, 100 animals instead of 300, no shadows, a grass field of about 2,000 tufts over 50 units instead of 8,800 over 78, a LOD ceiling of 250 m instead of 400 m, six clouds instead of twelve, and a pixel ratio of at most 1.5 instead of 2. Everything else is the same probe: the same terrain, the same sea, the same sky, the same groups, the same cards, and the same controls. On a phone viewport the worker builds a low patch in about 0.3 s, and a forest site of 6,000 plants and 91 animals draws in about 1.3 ms of card time at 30 m over the ground.
- Every mesh, every card, and every render target of the ground goes away with the probe. `Ground.dispose()` drops the listeners and the controls and walks the whole ground group, and the plants and the animals free their own meshes and the targets the cards were baked into. `renderer.info.memory` therefore returns to its orbit numbers after a recall and after a new world, however many times the reader goes down and comes back.
- The app holds one mode: orbit, descending, ground, or ascending. The globe scene stays in memory in every mode. In ground mode the globe is not drawn and none of its per-frame work runs, so the ground gets the whole frame. See `docs/probe.md` for the decisions behind the probe: the two scales, the landing site, the patch, the frame budget, and the ground camera.
- `species.js` rolls a set of species per world: class, niche, body plan, limbs, head, extras, gait, movement, colours, and modular lore. It runs inside the worker.
- `fauna.js` builds a creature from a genome, rigs it for the vertex shader, steers it, and runs the inspector card.
- A legged animal walks on a gait clock that runs off the ground it covers, not off the wall clock. A foot holds the ground for two thirds of the cycle and sweeps back at a constant rate while the body passes over it, then it lifts, folds at the knee, and swings forward again on a cubic that leaves the sweep at the speed it entered it. The leg extends through the stance, so the arc about the hip cannot lift the planted foot. The cycle rate is the speed divided by the stride, and the stride is the sweep of the foot, so a fast animal takes the same steps more often. A quad of 4.5 m used to slide its planted foot 2.4 m per cycle; it now slides 4 cm. A quad also walks the four-beat lateral sequence a horse walks, left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore, so three feet always carry it. An insect keeps its alternating tripod, and a serpent locks the wave along its body to the ground in the same way.
- An animal turns as its body allows. It cannot walk a circle tighter than 1.3 of its own length, and a flyer needs three times that, so a turn tighter than the circle makes it slow down instead. It leans into the turn by the sideways pull, it looks where it turns before its body follows, and the legs on the inside of the turn take the shorter stride. Its tail swings with the stride, and its body rises once over every footfall and rocks from side to side once a stride.
- `phenomena.js` draws the one natural activity a world can have: the glow, smoke, and embers of a volcano, the jet of a geyser, the light of a fissure, the curtains of an aurora, or the bolts of a thunderstorm. The worker picks the kind and the site, shapes and paints the ground, and keeps flora and fauna away from it.
- `music.js` plays a chip-tune for each world with the Web Audio API. The world type picks a mode, a chord progression, the tempo range, the swing, the lead timbre, and the wind. The day length and the gravity set the tempo and the root note. The temperature sets the tone. The song is a 32-bar loop: an intro, a question and answer phrase, a bridge, the phrase again with variation, and a short outro. The melody follows a 1/f noise contour, so it wanders like a tune, and it snaps to the chord on strong beats. The bass moves against the lead and approaches each new chord by a step. A pad holds the chords, and light percussion keeps the beat. Small timing jitter, accents by beat, a crescendo into each phrase, legato and staccato notes, a small reverb, and an echo keep it from a machine feel. The song fades in, and a new world crossfades. The music starts muted, so a new visitor gets silence until they press the speaker button. The volume and the mute state persist in `localStorage`. Browsers start the sound after the first tap or key press. On iOS a looping silent audio element starts with the same tap, so the music also plays with the ring switch on silent.
- Continents come from three low-frequency noise octaves, so each world gets a few large landmasses with bays and peninsulas, not a spray of islands. Each type has a target land fraction, Earth has 29%, and the sea level is the quantile of the terrain field that leaves that fraction dry. Fine relief fades out at the coast so it cannot cut the shore into specks. A masked ridge term adds a few volcanic island chains. Ocean worlds keep their archipelagos.
- Worlds are stored as seeds, not meshes. A saved world regenerates in about a second and the store stays small.
- Small or coarse-pointer devices get a lower mesh detail, fewer plants, and no shadows.
- The globe is a stylised miniature. A plant and a creature are small against the sphere, so a forest reads as a forest and not as a row of towers. A high-tier world places up to 10,500 plants, and a lush terran world fills that budget. The ocean shimmers on a period of about 14 seconds, so no swell travels across the water.

## Controls

Drag to spin and tilt. Scroll or pinch to zoom. Zoom in close and the view tilts toward the horizon. Get close to the ground to find the wildlife.

Press "Send a probe to the surface" in the sidebar, or the 🛸 button in the sidebar header, and a message asks you to select the area. A square then marks the cell under the pointer. The square is the true footprint of the ground the probe brings back, it snaps to the cell grid, it takes a creature that lives in the cell, and the planet holds still so the site stays put. Zoom in first to see the square, because one cell is only a few pixels wide from far out. A click or a tap sends the probe to that cell. A drag turns the planet and sends nothing. Escape and a second press of the button cancel. The share button copies the URL of the view you are looking at. A gas giant has no ground, so it gets no probe and no square.

The view dives at the site, fades to the colour of the atmosphere, and the ground appears. Press "Recall the probe" to come back to orbit over the same site. The zoom no longer carries the reader between the two places: it stops at the limit, and the limit offers the journey. Zoom out to the ceiling of 500 m on the ground and a half lit "Recall the probe" fades in at the foot of the screen; zoom all the way in on the globe and "Send a probe to the surface" does the same. The button only shows where the zoom has nothing left to give, so it never covers a view the reader is still moving. The URL carries the whole view, so a link opens the same ground from the same camera. It reads `#Seed/lat,lon,dist` in orbit, where the three numbers are the point of the globe under the camera and its distance in globe radii, for example `#Auralis/16.69,-13.78,1.450`. While the probe is down it reads `#Seed@lat,lon/x,z,dist,az,pol`: the site, then the target x and z metres from it, then the distance in metres, the azimuth, and the polar angle of the camera, for example `#Auralis@-18.28,-8.91/180.0,-260.0,120.0,120.32,66.47`. The address bar follows the camera twice a second, so the link is always the view on the screen. The older forms `#Seed` and `#Seed@lat,lon` still work and take the camera a new world gets. Open a link with a site and the world lands the reader on the ground. A new seed, the dice, and a saved world always return to orbit. A gas giant never gets a probe.

On the ground the controls change, because the reader no longer turns a planet but stands on one. Moving takes the first gesture. Drag with the left button, or with one finger, to grab the ground and pull yourself over it. Hold the same press still, and you keep walking that way until you let go; steer with the pointer, because the walk follows where it points. The keys walk too: W A S D or the arrow keys, and Shift runs. Q and E turn the view, R and F tilt it, and + and - zoom, so a reader with only a keyboard owns every part of the view. Looking takes the second gesture: drag with the right button, or with two fingers, to turn the view. Scroll or pinch to zoom. The pan and the walk both slide the target over the terrain, the target holds 1 m over the ground, and both stop 450 m from the site where the fog starts. The walk slows over the last 80 m instead of meeting a wall, and only the part of the step that goes outward slows, so you still walk along the edge and back in at full speed. The height of the camera sets the speeds: near the ground a wheel step moves a metre or two and a walk covers 11 m a second, at the ceiling a step moves about a hundred and a walk covers 150. The height also sets the tilt. From the ceiling down to 60 m the view turns from the patch below to the horizon. Under 60 m the tilt is yours, and it runs 30 degrees over the horizon, so you can look up at the canopy, the clouds, and the animals that fly. The camera holds 2 m over the terrain and 500 m over the site. A click or a tap on the ground glides the view to that point in 0.8 s, and a camera more than 200 m from its target also comes a third of the way in. A tap on an animal glides to the animal and marks it with a ring. A button at the foot of the screen then offers its card, so a tap always moves the view and the card never blocks the way. A tap on the ground, or Escape, takes the mark off. A drag never glides.

All controls live in one sidebar: the seed input, the current world with its stats and fauna, the share button, the saved worlds, and the music volume. The speaker button in the sidebar header mutes the music. The 🛸 button beside it sends and recalls the probe. It does what the text button does, and it stays in reach while the sidebar is folded. The header button folds the sidebar to one line that shows the current world. On a phone the sidebar docks at the bottom as a sheet, and a tap on the planet folds it away. The sheet holds one scroll region, so it brings the probe button into view when it opens and when the button changes what it says. Press `/` to focus the seed input and `Escape` to close the creature inspector.

## Natural activity

About two worlds in three have one phenomenon, never more. The world type sets the odds of each kind: a volcano with a cone, a crater, smoke and embers; a geyser that erupts every ten seconds or so; a long ragged fissure with side branches that walks across the land and stops at the shore; an aurora that folds around one pole; or a thunderstorm cell where bolts strike down from the atmosphere in two or three strokes, each with a new shape. Ice worlds get cryovolcanoes, cryogeysers, and crevasses. Gas giants get lightning in the great storm or an aurora. The info panel shows the activity of the world.

## Fauna

Every world rolls its own species, two to four of them, from the seed. Each species is a genome:

- a class: land, air, or sub-surface, chosen to fit a niche (meadow, forest, beach, lowland, dune, snow, ash, open sea, cloud deck);
- a locomotion: monopod, biped, tripod, quadruped, hexapod, serpent; gas sac, wings, fins; or a breathing arch, a periscope neck, or a plough that swims under the ground;
- a body plan: blob, spindle, chain, dome, disc, or a swarm of shards around a core;
- a head: beak, mandibles, eye stalks, lure, crest, tusks, or none;
- extras: sail, spikes, lamp beads, tendrils, garden, plates, tail, flukes, antennae, mounds;
- a sociality: solitary, a pair, or a herd of four to fourteen, with the size of the formation in metres.

The geometry is built from those parts. Every vertex carries a rig record (mode, phase, amplitude, weight) and a pivot, and one shader animates all species: legs swing about the hip and fold at the knee on the forward stroke, wing sheets roll at the root and bend toward the tip, tendrils and tails sway, lures and beads pulse, heads nod, flukes lag the body. A per-species carriage moves the whole body: a walk bob, a hop, a wave, a float, an arch pulse, or a rise and sink. Legs only swing while the animal actually moves.

The lore is modular too. The name, the binomial, the habitat, the size, the diet, the manner, and the story are all assembled from the parts the animal really has, so a lantern-headed tripod waits in ambush and a plated hexapod licks minerals from the rock. The manner and the story also say how the animal groups: a grazer moves in herds of twelve, a jawed strider hunts in pairs, and a serpent keeps to itself. The size text gives the real size of the animal in metres, and the ground scale uses the same number.

See `docs/fauna.md` for the architecture: the genome fields, the rig modes, the carriages, and the placement rules.

Creatures roam on procedural paths. Two slow oscillators with per-creature random frequencies steer each one, a leash pulls it back toward its home spot, and grazers stop and start on a third oscillator. Land creatures follow a coarse height map from the worker and turn back at the shoreline. Click a creature, or a species chip in the info card, to open the inspector: a live turntable with the path trace and the species backstory.

On the ground the same creatures walk in groups at their real size. One mover carries the group, and the members hold a formation around it, so a herd of nine crosses a hill as one body and a solitary animal roams alone. The size text of the species is the size of the animal against the terrain: a quad of "4.5 m at the shoulder" stands 4.5 m tall over a grid of 2 m squares. A creature close to the reader is the full body, and one at distance is a coarse body of under 80 triangles that the same shader animates. See the "Ground tier" section of `docs/fauna.md`.
