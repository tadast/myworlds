# My Worlds

Type a name or a word. Get a small low-poly planet. The same word always gives the same world.

Static site: HTML, CSS, and JavaScript modules. No build step and no backend. The site needs no Node; only the checks in `tools/` run in Node.

## Run locally

Serve the directory over HTTP. Web Workers do not run from `file://`.

```sh
ruby -rwebrick -e 's=WEBrick::HTTPServer.new(:Port => 5555, :DocumentRoot => Dir.pwd).start'
```

Open <http://localhost:5555/>. Add `#YourName` to the URL to open a specific world.

## Deploy to GitHub Pages

Push the repository and enable Pages for the branch root. `.nojekyll` keeps Jekyll away from `vendor/`.

## Checks

The checks run in Node, with no browser and no install. Run them after a change to generation, to the cell grid, or to the landing:

```sh
node tools/world-checksum.mjs --check
node tools/cell-grid-check.mjs
node tools/frame-check.mjs
node tools/carrier-check.mjs
node tools/carrier-fix-check.mjs
node tools/chapters-check.mjs
node tools/story-check.mjs
node tools/probe-check.mjs
node tools/patch-terrain-check.mjs
node tools/ruin-check.mjs
node tools/way-check.mjs
node tools/lore-audit/audit.mjs
node tools/lore-audit/way-sample.mjs --check 300
```

- `world-checksum` drives `worker.js` itself, on both device tiers of `tiers.js`, and hashes every world and every patch against `tools/world-checksum.baseline.txt`. It also fails when the cell of the source holds no wreck, when another cell holds one, or when a phenomenon stands off its cell. The last column of a line hashes the lore and nothing else, so a change of wording moves that column and a change of the ground moves the others. A change that must leave the worlds alone must match it. Record a new baseline only for a change that must move the worlds, and state in the commit which hashes move and why.
- `cell-grid-check` tests the interface of `cell-grid.js`: the round trip of a cell, the shared edges, the arc of a cell, the box and its inverse, the hand of the box, the frame of a site, and the site of every cell.
- `frame-check` and `carrier-check` test the box against the frame of the sky, the needle, and the bearing; `carrier-check` runs every test with the wreck and with the ruin as the source. `carrier-fix-check` tests the wedges of the search, in both chapters, and the two pins the finds stand on the globe. These three import modules of the page, which take three.js by a bare name; `tools/three-hook.mjs` is the one resolve hook that points the name at `vendor/`.
- `chapters-check` walks whole stories through `chapters.js` with no browser: a scripted reader lands, takes fixes, reads the brief, finds the wreck, tunes, and finds the ruin, and the check reads the words of the search in `view().row`, the marks of the saved worlds, the levels of the two motifs, and the store at each step. It tests the strict order, the rule of the search the receiver follows over every record of the two searches, the records of older builds, a bad hand, a full store, and the bound of the seeds.
- `story-check` walks a whole story through `story.js` with no browser, in orbit and on the ground. At each step it reads the two rules of `docs/ui.md`: a closed chapter tells nothing of itself, and no word names a wreck before the find. It also reads the objective at each stage of a landing, a gas giant, the counts in words, and the store of `field-guide.js`.
- `patch-terrain-check` builds patches through `worker.js` on the cell of the wreck, of the ruin, and of the phenomenon, and on plain cells, and tests `patch-terrain.js` against them: every node, points on the triangles the mesh draws, and the discs of the keep-out. No plant and no group of the worker stands on a disc, so the ground reads the discs the worker masked.
- `probe-check` walks every change of a landing through `probe.js` with no browser, on a clock it moves by hand: the descent that waits for the patch, the guard when no patch comes, the ascent that gives the fix of the landing at its end, the jump of chapter 3 from the ground to the twin, the calls a probe refuses, and an abort from each of the four modes.
- `ruin-check` builds 500 worlds through `worker.js` and tests the ruin of phase 2: the hashes of `ruin-types.js`, the place against its band and its tests, the compass word against `bearingTo()`, the maker, and one ruin on both tiers. It also builds the patch of the ruin on 70 of those worlds, every proto among them, and tests the flat disc, the reach, the plants and the herds off the disc, and the body on it. It tests the card of the ruin on every world: the rows, the makers against the species, the day and the ship of the call, and the glyphs of the way on. It tests the traces of the crew on the patches: a camp or a cairn by who went, on the side of the wreck, off the body, inside the soft edge, on a flat pad, with no plant on it. It prints how many worlds fell back to a wider band and names them. It takes one to four minutes, by the load of the machine; `--seeds 50` runs a short pass. See `docs/ruin.md`.
- `way-check` tests chapter 3 on 300 worlds: the rules of `way-types.js` (the call sign, the send, the fates, the forms, and the graves), the twin against its band and the tests of the ruin, the herd and the kin, the fate against the crew, the ends of the people of the third log against the table of the fate, the rescue line, the card of the twin, and the patch of the twin on every twelfth world: the stones, the tent by the ends of the people, and the herd. `lore-audit/way-sample.mjs --check 300` reads the words of the third log under every fate. See `docs/issues/p3-00-the-way-on.md`.
- `tools/story-lab.html` is the lab of the story. Open `http://localhost:5555/tools/story-lab.html#audit-2`: it builds the world in the page and shows the log of the wreck, the second log, and the third log under each of the six fates side by side, with the fate of the seed marked. A Play link on each fate opens the game on that seed with `?fate=<id>&chapter=3`, so the reader can walk that timeline on the ground. The scan lists the audit seeds with a crew at the ruin, by fate, form, type, and crew. `node tools/lore-audit/way-sample.mjs 0 3 --fate all` prints the same timelines in the terminal.
- `lore-audit/audit.mjs` sweeps every lore permutation, the second log at the ruin among them, and with `--seeds 200` it reads the two logs of every world against each other. See "Fauna" and "Flora" below, and "The checks" in `docs/ruin.md`.

## How it works

- `generate.js` turns the seed into a hash, a PRNG, and seeded simplex noise. It builds an icosphere, raises terrain, paints biomes per face, places flora and fauna, and condenses clouds. It exports two calls, the world call and the patch call, and each returns its result. A third export, `placeFacts()`, serves the Node checks only. The patch call takes a site and builds the whole cell the site falls in: it finds the cell, the width of the cell, and whether the cell holds the phenomenon or the source, from the world of its own seed. `worker.js` runs them off the main thread as a module worker and reports progress, and the Node tools in `tools/` import `generate.js` directly. The lore files are ES modules, and both sides import them.
- `app.js` renders the result with three.js (flat-shaded vertex colours, instanced flora, fauna and clouds, atmosphere shaders, rings, moons). It saves each world's seed, type, and a thumbnail to `localStorage`.
- `deck.js` draws the interface over the planet: the title, the dock, the window, and the chrome of the planet. `story.js` writes the words of the Story window and of the objective, and `field-guide.js` keeps the field guide. Neither of the two holds a DOM node, so `tools/story-check.mjs` reads them in Node. See "Controls" below and `docs/ui.md`.
- A gas giant has no surface. The gas gets thicker with depth, and the clouds condense in decks at fixed pressures, so the globe draws two decks and no skin. The banded body at radius 1 is the lower deck, and it lies in shade. A shader moves weather over its bands. The bands streak and drift east and west, and thin, fast streaks run along the equator. At each pole, the jet bends into a polygon of five to nine sides, as the hexagon on Saturn does, and a spiral turns in the dark eye inside it. The great storm and three to six small storms turn their spiral arms, each the way its hemisphere turns it. Each pattern turns as a whole, because a flow that turns faster at one latitude than at the next tears a pattern into noise in a few minutes. The small storms and the sides of the polygons take a stream of their own, so no world changes. A shell at radius 1.036 is the upper deck. A shader streaks it along the bands, and alternate bands flow east and west. The zones hold the clouds, and the belts between them keep only a thin haze, so the reader sees the lower deck through a belt. The deck opens over the great storm and thins to nothing at the limb. Past the limb, the haze of each pixel thins by e for each 0.03 of the height where the view ray passes closest to the centre. The edge of the world is therefore soft, and a low camera sees a glowing horizon. The whales and the other flyers swim in the clear gas between the two decks. Each one keeps its own clock of 55 to 110 s, rises through the upper deck, and sinks back, and its nose follows the climb. A whale under the deck shows veiled and pale. The clock comes from the phase of the animal and draws no numbers, so no wander changes.
- Every world carries the tilt of its axis, and the tilt decides the light and the climate. The roll follows what is known of real planets: about a fifth stand nearly upright, half hold a modest tilt around 14 degrees, a quarter lie over on their side, and a few turn the other way. Measured over 4,000 seeds, 68% stand under 30 degrees, which is the share the planets of the Sun hold. The season the world stands in gives the latitude the star stands over, and the planet turns to meet it, so the terminator, the tilt, and the ice the reader sees all agree. The temperature of a latitude is the light it takes: the mean over the year sets the climate, and the day of the season moves it, so ground that stands in the dark through the whole turn is cold and ground that holds a midnight sun is warm. Measured on `Vesper`, a world with a mean of 28 °C and a tilt of 138 degrees, the site at 62 N stands in the polar night at -8.7 °C and the site at 62 S holds the sun at 38.9 °C. A frozen world stays frozen whatever its tilt does.
- `star.js` rolls the star of a world from its own hash of the seed, so no world changes. The odds follow the census of the stars near the Sun: about 70% red dwarfs, 12% orange dwarfs, 8% yellow dwarfs, 6% white dwarfs, 3% F stars, and under 1% A and B stars, red giants, and pulsars. About 31% of the systems are binaries, and the chance of a companion rises with the mass of the primary. The star stands 80 units out along the sun direction, as a billboard with a disc, a corona, and rays. A red giant shows slow convection cells. A pulsar sweeps two beams round its spin axis and flares when a beam points at the camera; a turn takes at least 1.1 s, so it never flashes more than twice a second. A binary pair runs a Kepler orbit of 90 to 240 s, and the star behind its partner goes dark. The star tints the light of the globe and of the ground. `?star=pulsar` (or `O` `B` `A` `F` `G` `K` `M` `giant` `dwarf` `brown`) and `&binary=1` or `&binary=0` force the system.
- `site.js` picks the landing site for the probe: a ray from the pointer onto the surface, a snap to the cell grid, a pull to a creature that lives in the cell or to the phenomenon of the world, a square marker on the ground, and the URL codec for the site and the camera. The cells come from a cube grid in `cell-grid.js`, which the worker and the page import: six faces of 157 by 157 quads, with the gnomonic coordinate warped through a tangent so a quad at the corner of a face holds about the arc of a quad at its middle. A cell is about 0.01 units of arc across, about 74 km on a 7,352 km planet, and it is the same size on the screen for every planet. The globe draws its surface from an icosphere at detail 100, which puts about 0.011 units between two vertices, so a smaller square would sit inside one facet and the coast the reader aims at would not be where the field puts it. A band of latitude used to carry the cells, and its step of longitude changed at every band, so a cell in one band did not line up with a cell in the next; the cells of a cube grid tile the whole globe, share their edges exactly, and hold no pole. The URL still keeps a lat and a lon, and it is the middle of the cell. The square is the true footprint of the patch, so what it holds is what the ground shows. The square is paint in the shader of the terrain and of the sea, so it lies on the same facets the reader sees. The pick meets those facets too: it walks the ray through buckets of facets inside the shell of the terrain, so the cell under the pointer is the cell the square paints at every camera angle. The pull to life stays inside that cell.
- Four of the land animals travel in bursts, and the world is what moves them. A monopod crouches and hops. A roller folds its legs and its head into its hull, drops on to the ball of its own body, and rolls: the ground it covers is what spins it, so it never skids, and it goes further downhill than up and will not start up a steep rise at all. A flow lurks still on a high point for most of a minute, lets go of its shape, runs the fall line as a flat sheet with its lamps at full and takes what the ground gives while it is spread, stands back up as a column where the sheet stopped, and hops the whole of itself back up the rise; it covers no ground at all while it holds a shape. A slinger hooks a cord on a real plant, hauls its body back, and lets go on the far side of it; with nothing in reach it crawls. Each one picks a heading while it charges and holds that heading through the whole throw, so none of them turns in the air. One mover carries all four, and it keeps books: it banks the ground the cruise speed asks for and pays the whole debt with one throw, so a species covers the ground its lore says it does however far a single throw carries it.
- `ground.js` holds the ground the probe lands on. The globe is a miniature at 1 unit for the planet radius. The ground is true scale: 1 unit is 1 metre. It keeps a second scene, a second camera, and second controls, and it draws with the same renderer. A sky dome of 5,000 m takes the atmosphere colour, and fog in the same colour runs from 300 m to 1,200 m at the ground on a high tier and from 450 m to 750 m on a low tier, so the horizon fades and the patch shows no hard edge. The long fog of the high tier hazes the whole depth of the view, so the far hills stay in it as shapes and the view does not end in a wall. The fog opens with the height of the camera, out to 1,500 m on a high tier and 1,325 m on a low tier at the ceiling of 500 m, so the reader looks over the patch and not down on it. The plants stop where the fog is solid, at every height, so no plant stops in clear air. The ground runs on to 3,150 m from the site, well past the fog, so the reader never sees where it ends.
- The ground patch is the cell the reader picked, drawn into a box 3,000 units square. The box lands on the quad of the cube grid, so the box is the cell. The box is right-handed, x along the u axis of the quad and z against its v axis, so the ground is the true image of the cell and the sky stands the right way round over it; `node tools/frame-check.mjs` fails on a mirror. The cell is far wider than the box, so the ground holds an artificial scale: one unit is tens of metres across, and one vertical scale serves the whole world, set so the highest land of that world stands 800 units over the sea. A cell that holds a range therefore reads tall and a cell on a plain reads flat, and two patches side by side agree on how tall a unit is. Plants and animals keep their lore size in units, so they read as normal against the ground and they are no longer the metres the lore text says. The trade is deliberate: a patch of 1,500 true metres is under 2 px on the globe at the closest zoom, so the reader could not aim at a coast, and a coast almost never crossed a window that narrow. The worker builds it while the probe falls, and the dive holds the screen until the reply lands. A height grid with a vertex every 2 units, or every 4 on a low tier, carries the globe field read on a grid of 65 by 65 across the cell and interpolated, plus the relief field below. The globe field used to come in as one linear tilt, because over 1,500 m the field is a plane; a cell bends inside it and a coast can cross it, so the field is now read per cell. The temperature and the moisture bend with it, so one patch can hold a beach, a meadow, and a forest, and three species instead of one. The colours follow the biome rules of the globe, so the ground reads in the colours the reader saw from orbit, with rock on the steep slopes and a lightness jitter per vertex. `ground.js` turns the grid into indexed flat-shaded meshes, and adds a rim: a second grid at 50 units per cell that carries the same globe field and the long waves of the same relief field out to 4,000 units from the site. The rim used to hold the height of the patch edge and drag it outward, which combed the edge into streaks; now the relief and the coast run on across the join. One mesh and one material draw the whole rim, and four dense strips tie it to the patch so the join holds no crack. The patch fades the waves a rim cell cannot carry out over its last two rim cells, and it thins its plants away over its last 100 units, because the rim carries none.
- The relief under the globe field carries the shape of the ground from the cell down to the metre. The globe holds nothing under about a fortieth of its radius: its finest octave runs at 36 turns over the sphere, so a cell of 60 km carries a third of one wave, and measured over 300 land cells the field inside a cell stands within 7% of a plane. The patch used to add three octaves of smooth noise off its own seed, which gave rounded blobs and gave two neighbouring patches different hills. It now adds a ridged multifractal of five octaves, from 1,800 units down to about 97, with each octave hung on the crest of the one above so a range grows spurs and a valley floor stays smooth, and a fine stack of three octaves for the ground at the feet. How much of the ridged stack the ground takes comes from the mountain term of the globe and from the height over the sea, so a range is rough and a plain rolls gently. Measured on `Auralis`, a patch holds 403 units from its lowest vertex to its highest on a range, 309 in forested hills, 204 on tundra, 101 on lowland, and 51 on a plain, where every patch held 240 before. The whole field reads the direction on the sphere and nothing else, and no seed of a patch enters it, so two patches that share an edge read one height along it: measured over 751 vertices of a join, they differ by 0.0002 units against an edge 1,000 units up, which is the rounding of a `Float32Array`. That is what a stream would need to stitch them. The camera stays 2 m above the terrain and at most 500 m above the site, and its target stays inside the fog start. Between the floor and the ceiling the reader owns the height: the camera flies in straight lines and does not ride the relief. The view turns over the horizon, up to the angle that holds the horizon on the bottom edge of the frame, so the reader can look up at the sky and at what flies in it. The eye keeps its floor there and the target rises instead, so the pivot of an up-view stands in the sky and the reader turns the head without walking. The probe arrives 450 m up and 884 m south of the site, so the reader sees the patch first and then zooms in. The ceiling comes from the fog: the fog opens 1.15 m for each metre of height, but the far edge of the box moves away only by the horizontal distance of the camera from the site, so the detail of the patch stops inside the fog and reads as a rectangle whenever the view looks down more steeply than 48 degrees. The reveal arrives at that tilt and the ceiling holds the height, so the rectangle stays hidden unless the reader climbs to 500 m and turns the view out at the horizon on purpose.
- **Flora and fauna are container words, and neither is an Earth word.** Flora is anything alive that stays in one place, and it does not have to be a plant: of the sixteen kinds only four are woody, six glow, three breathe, and the rest are fungal bodies, crystals, a stone, a whip, a blade, a turf, and a stack of balanced plates. Fauna is anything alive that moves under its own power, and it does not have to be an animal: a body may be a swarm of many small bodies, a sac that floats and never lands, a plough that travels under the surface, or a whale that swims in the air of a gas giant. The code says "plant" and "animal" because one short word is necessary for one body in an array. Read them as "one stationary body" and "one body that travels". `docs/flora.md` and `docs/fauna.md` hold the rule that every new kind and every new line of text must follow.
- `ground-flora.js` grows the plants on that ground, and `flora-geometry.js` holds the shape of one plant for both the globe and the ground. The worker places up to 20,000 plants over the patch, or 6,000 on a low tier. One cell of 6 units takes at most one plant, the plant jitters inside its cell, and a plant closer than 3 units to a neighbour is dropped. A cell below sea level, on a beach, or on a slope steeper than 0.9 takes nothing, except that rock and crystal may stand on a slope.
- The patch holds sixteen kinds of plant. The globe grows the first seven: tree, pine, cactus, crystal, mushroom, boulder, and palm. The ground adds nine alien kinds: a tower mushroom that stands over any tree, a spindle of bare whips with a bud at each tip, a sac that breathes in and out, a crystal that pushes out of the ground, a tuft of grass, a colossus, a ribbed fan, a stalk of hung pods, and a stack of balanced plates. A kind carries a style: how much it glows, how far the wind bends it, how far it breathes, how far out it must stay a mesh, and how far one plant of that kind may lean and spread against the next.
- Every world holds a flora signature, so two planets never grow the same plants. The signature is a hash of the seed. It moves seven numbers before a kind lays out its shape: how thick a stalk stands, how wide a crown spreads, how flat a cap sits, how many parts a plant of many parts carries, how far the parts lean out, whether the plant carries a node that glows, and how far the derived hues turn from the palette. So the tower mushroom of one world is a slim spire and the tower mushroom of the next is a squat parasol, and a spindle carries four whips here and nine there. The signature also picks the kinds: a world borrows one to three from the whole alien set and drops up to two its type usually grows, so two temperate worlds hold different lists. The globe reads the same signature, so the plants a reader sees from orbit are the plants the ground shows.
- Four noise fields shape a patch, so the ground is not one even carpet. The community field says which kinds live where: a patch rolls four to seven communities, each with a lead kind of its own, a companion, a density, and a vigour, and two fields at two wavelengths lay them over the box as a patchwork. The grove field of 46 units knots them into thickets. The bare field of 195 units opens ground that holds nothing. The vigour field of 165 units says how big they grow, so one part of the box stands stunted and another stands tall. A size comes off a curve that bunches at the small end, with a rare giant, and it stops at 1.9 of the top of the range of its kind, so only a colossus reaches the size of a colossus.
- Two passes run after the scan and both keep their plants past the cap, because the cap must not drop a landmark. The arrangements stand seven to nineteen plants of one kind in a ring, an arc, a row, or a spiral, so the reader asks who made them. The colossus pass drops one to three bodies of 70 to 150 units, each with a court of smaller plants, spaced at least 340 units apart.
- Every plant carries its own lean, its own width against its height, and its own tint, all from the hash of its index. The tint rides on `instanceColor`, which the LOD walk copies beside the matrix, and the card quad takes a white colour attribute so the same tint multiplies the baked picture. The wind and the breath run in the vertex shader before the instance matrix, so both take the lean, the width, and the size of the instance for free.
- `ground-cover.js` stands blades of grass and small stones on the ground around the camera, out to 32 m on a high tier and 24 m on a low tier. The blades grow where the pattern of `ground-detail.js` paints cover, up to 150 per square metre, and the stones lie where it paints sand, ash, bare rock, or snow; a lava world lies thick with cinders. The graphics card places both: the vertex shader reads the height, the colour, and the biome of the patch from textures and takes the place of each blade from a hash of its tile, so the CPU only writes the list of tiles in view, 2 m on a side. A blade stands on the exact triangle of the terrain, takes the colour of the ground under it with a dark root and a light tip, takes the light of the facet, and bends with gusts that run over the field with the wind. The cover thins, shrinks, and turns to the colour of the ground with distance, so its edge never shows, and a far tile draws a quarter of the blades. At 1280 by 800 and a pixel ratio of 2 it costs about 0.7 to 2 ms of the frame on the development Mac. The pattern carries the detail past it.
- A plant is a real size in units: a tree 5 to 16, a pine 7 to 22, a palm 6 to 14, a cactus 2 to 6, a crystal 1.5 to 8, a mushroom 1 to 4, a boulder 0.8 to 5, a tower mushroom 19 to 42, a spindle 4 to 26, a sac 1.5 to 7, a growing crystal 2 to 12, a fan 3 to 10, a pod 2 to 9, a stack 3 to 15, and a colossus 70 to 150.
- The plants draw at two levels of detail. A plant within the LOD distance of the camera is the full mesh. That distance starts at 150 m and the frame time moves it; see the adaptive LOD below. The style of a kind multiplies it, because a card cannot carry a body that fills the screen: a colossus takes 14 and stays a mesh out to the fog, a tower mushroom takes 3, and a tuft of grass takes 0.3. A plant beyond it is a card: one vertical quad that carries a picture of the same mesh, baked once per kind at patch load into a target of 96 by 96 pixels, 160 for the tower, 192 for the colossus, and 48 for a tuft of grass, with the colours and the sun of the site.
- The card also holds a floor of its own, and the knob cannot pull a plant inside it. A plant `s` units tall and `d` units away covers `s * focal / d` pixels, where `focal` is the height of the view over twice the tangent of half the field of view. A tile of the card holds `px` pixels across `frame` plant heights, so past `s * frame * focal / px` the picture is no longer magnified and the swap is invisible; nearer than that the reader sees a blown-up picture and calls the plant flat. The knob alone used to decide, and on a 30 Hz display it fell to its floor of 40 m and put 8,018 of 8,126 plants on cards, some of them ten metres away. The floor is per plant, not per kind, because one kind holds sizes from 5 units to 40. It costs one multiply and one compare in the walk, which still runs in 0.2 ms for 8,000 plants. A card is an impostor: the bake draws each kind from 25 directions over the upper half of the sphere, 16 bearings on the horizon, 8 at about 50 degrees, and one straight down, into one texture of the colour and one of the direction of the surface. The card is a rectangle about the middle of the plant that faces the eye, and the shader reads the three views nearest the direction of the eye in the frame of that one plant, carried along the eye onto the picture of each view so the three line up. The card takes the spin of its plant, so it shows the side the mesh shows, and a plant under a camera that passes over it holds still instead of turning to face the camera. The card is a Lambert material, and the direction of the surface from the texture replaces the normal of the flat rectangle, so the sun and the sky light the card as they light the mesh, at every hour and from every side. The card takes an alpha test and no blending, so it writes depth and sorts with the terrain. Every frame one walk reads the distance of every plant and writes it to the near mesh or to the card mesh. Inside a band of ±12% around that distance it writes the plant to both, and a 4 by 4 dither on the screen shares the pixels of the plant between the two, so a plant turns from a card into a mesh over a distance and never in one frame. The shadow of the mesh takes the same dither in the shadow pass, so it comes in with the mesh. The matrices are built once at load and the walk only copies them, so it costs about 0.4 ms for 20,000 plants. A forest site at 30 m over the ground draws in about 4.4 ms of card time, terrain included.
- `ground-fauna.js` puts the animals on that ground, at the size their lore states and in the groups their sociality gene asks for. The worker rolls 10 to 30 groups per patch, up to 300 animals, from the species whose niche lies within 300 m of the site, and the species the site was pulled to is always there. One mover carries the anchor of each group over a leash of 60 to 200 m at 0.5 to 6 m/s, and the members hold their place in the formation, turn to face the way they travel, and stand on the terrain, tilted to its slope. A flyer holds 12 to 40 m above the ground under it, so it clears a hill, and it rises and falls 1.4 m on a slow clock of its own. A flyer also gets three rules the walkers do not need, because the sky holds nothing to measure it against: it keeps its full mesh 2.5 times as far out, it never draws narrower than 11 px, and it lays a soft shadow on the ground under it at the slant of the sun. The shadow is the cue that makes the reader look up. A serpent and a plough follow the path of the anchor with a lag, so the chain reads. The whole herd stops and starts together, because every speed in the group comes from one anchor, but each animal keeps its own speed inside the formation, and its legs read that. No member travels faster than 1.35 of its cruise speed, so a wide formation swinging round a turn stretches instead of dragging its outer animals sideways. The test for the animal under the pointer takes the nearest one to the camera whose body holds the point, and it drops an animal that stands behind the hill the ray meets, so a tap never carries the reader off to an animal somewhere else. On a site with 299 animals the steering costs about 0.10 ms of the frame.
- The animals draw at two levels of detail, as the plants do. An animal within the same LOD distance is the full creature of 100 to 980 triangles. An animal beyond it is a coarse creature of 16 to 70 triangles, built from the same genome by the same builder: a ball becomes an octahedron, a tube becomes a three-sided open cylinder, a leg becomes one tapered bone with no knee, a chain body becomes one tapered tube, and the head furniture, the eyes, and every extra but the sail and the plates go. Each octahedron reaches exactly as far as the ball it replaces, and both levels take one scale from the full build, so a swap changes neither the size nor the place of an animal. The same shader animates both, with the leg swing at half the amplitude so a leg of a few pixels does not shimmer. The walk that places the animals also sorts them into the two meshes, with a band of 10% around that distance so an animal at the boundary cannot flicker, and the far mesh casts no shadow. - One knob keeps that target: the LOD distance of the ground. `Ground.update()` reads the clock every 500 ms. Over the target by 10% it multiplies the distance by 0.85. With the display at its refresh and the app under 70% of the frame it multiplies by 1.1, and it stays between 40 m and the ceiling of the device tier, 400 m or 250 m. The two steps read two numbers, because the display holds the interval at the refresh and the interval can therefore never show the room that is left. A step down also stops a step up for three seconds, so the knob cannot ring around the value where the machine sits exactly at the refresh. The plants and the animals both read the knob, and nothing else follows it: the shadows and the pixel ratio stay device-tier decisions. The settled distance goes to `localStorage` per tier, so the next landing starts near the right value. The sun casts only while the camera is lower than the knob over the ground, and that gate takes a band from 1.05 to 1.35 of the distance and holds each state for 1.5 s, so a knob that moves cannot switch the shadow on and off.
- Add `?perf` to the address for a small overlay, for example `http://localhost:5555/?perf#Auralis`. It shows the frame time, the work, the target and the refresh rate, the LOD distance, the near and far counts of the plants, the tuft count and the build time of the grass, the near and far counts of the animals, the shadow, the height of the camera, and the draw calls and triangles of the last frame. It reads twice a second, and it works in orbit as well, with the numbers of the globe. It stands at the top left, under the world chip, clear of the sound button and the objective. Without the flag the page builds no overlay and does no work for it.
- `tiers.js` holds the two rows of the device tier, and `app.js` picks one. A coarse pointer, a screen under 600 px on its short side, or four processor cores or fewer put the reader on the low tier. `Q` in `app.js` is the chosen row: `Q` is the globe and `Q.ground` is the probe, and every part reads the tier from there. The Node tools read the same rows, so a check measures the worlds a reader gets. A low device gets a probe with a height grid every 4 m instead of 2 m, 8,400 plants instead of 120,000, 100 animals instead of 300, no shadows, a grass field of about 2,000 tufts over 50 units instead of 8,800 over 78, the fog of 450 m to 750 m instead of 300 m to 1,200 m, a LOD ceiling of 250 m instead of 400 m, six clouds instead of twelve, and a pixel ratio of at most 1.5 instead of 2. Everything else is the same probe: the same terrain, the same sea, the same sky, the same groups, the same cards, and the same controls. On a phone viewport the worker builds a low patch in about 0.3 s, and a forest site of 6,000 plants and 91 animals draws in about 1.3 ms of card time at 30 m over the ground.
- Every mesh, every card, and every render target of the ground goes away with the probe. `Ground.dispose()` drops the listeners and the controls and walks the whole ground group, and the plants and the animals free their own meshes and the targets the cards were baked into. The walk also frees the shadow map of the sun, and `disposeDetail()` frees the stack of the fine pattern, so the next landing bakes it again in 9 to 20 ms. `renderer.info.memory` therefore returns to its orbit numbers after a recall and after a new world, however many times the reader goes down and comes back.
- The app holds one mode: orbit, descending, ground, or ascending. The globe scene stays in memory in every mode. In ground mode the globe is not drawn and none of its per-frame work runs, so the ground gets the whole frame. See `docs/probe.md` for the decisions behind the probe: the two scales, the landing site, the patch, the frame budget, and the ground camera.
- `lore.js` is the lore engine. It turns the numbers of a planet into a set of tags, picks text through gates written against those tags, and assembles a story out of named slots. It holds no animal words and no plant words, so the fauna and the flora share one engine. It runs inside the worker and on the page.
- `species.js` rolls a set of species per world: class, niche, body plan, limbs, head, extras, gait, movement, colours, and the fauna vocabulary the lore engine draws on. It runs inside the worker.
- `fauna.js` builds a creature from a genome, rigs it for the vertex shader, steers it, and runs the inspector card.
- `flora-lore.js` holds the flora vocabulary and writes the lore of every plant kind a patch grows. It runs inside the worker. `flora-card.js` draws the plant on the study card. See "Flora" below and `docs/flora.md`.
- A legged animal walks on a gait clock that runs off the ground it covers, not off the wall clock. A foot holds the ground for two thirds of the cycle and sweeps back at a constant rate while the body passes over it, then it lifts, folds at the knee, and swings forward again on a cubic that leaves the sweep at the speed it entered it. The leg extends through the stance, so the arc about the hip cannot lift the planted foot. The cycle rate is the speed divided by the stride, and the stride is the sweep of the foot, so a fast animal takes the same steps more often. A quad of 4.5 m used to slide its planted foot 2.4 m per cycle; it now slides 4 cm. A quad also walks the four-beat lateral sequence a horse walks, left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore, so three feet always carry it. An insect keeps its alternating tripod, and a serpent locks the wave along its body to the ground in the same way.
- An animal turns as its body allows. It cannot walk a circle tighter than 1.3 of its own length, and a flyer needs three times that, so a turn tighter than the circle makes it slow down instead. It leans into the turn by the sideways pull, it looks where it turns before its body follows, and the legs on the inside of the turn take the shorter stride. Its tail swings with the stride, and its body rises once over every footfall and rocks from side to side once a stride.
- `phenomena.js` draws the one natural activity a world can have: the glow, smoke, and embers of a volcano, the jet of a geyser, the light of a fissure, the curtains of an aurora, or the bolts of a thunderstorm. The worker picks the kind and the site, shapes and paints the ground, and keeps flora and fauna away from it.
- `music.js` plays a chip-tune for each world with the Web Audio API. The world type picks a mode, a chord progression, the tempo range, the swing, the lead timbre, and the wind. The day length and the gravity set the tempo and the root note. The temperature sets the tone. The song is a 32-bar loop: an intro, a question and answer phrase, a bridge, the phrase again with variation, and a short outro. The melody follows a 1/f noise contour, so it wanders like a tune, and it snaps to the chord on strong beats. The bass moves against the lead and approaches each new chord by a step. A pad holds the chords, and light percussion keeps the beat. Small timing jitter, accents by beat, a crescendo into each phrase, legato and staccato notes, a small reverb, and an echo keep it from a machine feel. The song fades in, and a new world crossfades. The music starts muted, so a new visitor gets silence until they press the sound button. The sound button and the Menu window set the volume, and the volume and the mute state persist in `localStorage`. Browsers start the sound after the first tap or key press. On iOS a looping silent audio element starts with the same tap, so the music also plays with the ring switch on silent.
- Continents come from three low-frequency noise octaves, so each world gets a few large landmasses with bays and peninsulas, not a spray of islands. Each type has a target land fraction, Earth has 29%, and the sea level is the quantile of the terrain field that leaves that fraction dry. Fine relief fades out at the coast so it cannot cut the shore into specks. A masked ridge term adds a few volcanic island chains. Ocean worlds keep their archipelagos.
- Worlds are stored as seeds, not meshes. A saved world regenerates in about a second and the store stays small.
- Small or coarse-pointer devices get a lower mesh detail, fewer plants, and no shadows.
- The globe is a stylised miniature. A plant and a creature are small against the sphere, so a forest reads as a forest and not as a row of towers. A high-tier world places up to 10,500 plants, and a lush terran world fills that budget. The ocean shimmers on a period of about 14 seconds, so no swell travels across the water.

## Controls

Drag to spin and tilt. Scroll or pinch to zoom. Zoom in close and the view tilts toward the horizon. Get close to the ground to find the wildlife.

The interface stands over the planet, and `docs/ui.md` describes it. An address with no world opens the title screen: a field for a name, the dice, and up to four saved worlds. Each new world in orbit opens with the arrival: the distress signal, a welcome back, or one card for a world with no story. On the planet the world chip stands top left, the objective top right, and the sound button in the top right corner. The dock at the foot is the only navigation. Its four tabs, Worlds, Story, Planet, and Menu, open one window, and the probe button stands raised in its middle.

The Worlds window holds a field for a new name with the dice, and every saved world as a card with its marks. The Story window holds the distress signal, the Now panel with the next step, and the chapters. The Planet window holds the globe, the facts, and the field guide. Its button "Copy a link to ‹world›" copies the URL of the view on the screen. The Menu window holds the sound and the volume, About, the title screen, and the controls. The sound button turns the music on and off, and a hover on it gives the volume.

Only one layer shows at a time: the card or a dialog, the window, the title, the arrival, or the planet. While the window stands open, the probe button reads "Close". Press `/` to open the Worlds window with the focus in the field of a new name. Escape closes one thing: the card, then the window or the arrival, then the mark, then the aim. A creature or a plant is undiscovered until the reader finds it on the planet and opens its card. The field guide shows an undiscovered one as a locked card that says "Undiscovered".

Press the probe button, "Send probe", to aim. A bar over the planet then says "Tap the planet where the probe should land", and a square marks the cell under the pointer. The square is the true footprint of the ground the probe brings back. It snaps to the cell grid and takes a creature that lives in the cell, and the planet holds still so the site stays put. Zoom in first to see the square, because one cell is only a few pixels wide from far out.

A click or a tap sends the probe to that cell. A drag turns the planet and sends nothing. While you aim, the probe button reads "Cancel". Escape, "Cancel" on the bar, and the probe button each cancel the aim. A gas giant has no ground: the probe button reads "No surface", and the planet takes no square.

The view dives at the site, fades to the colour of the atmosphere, and the ground appears. Press "Recall probe" in the dock to come back to orbit over the same site. The zoom does not carry the reader between the two places: it stops at the ceiling of 500 m on the ground and at the closest zoom on the globe. The URL carries the whole view, so a link opens the same ground from the same camera. It reads `#Seed/lat,lon,dist` in orbit, where the three numbers are the point of the globe under the camera and its distance in globe radii, for example `#Auralis/16.69,-13.78,1.450`. While the probe is down it reads `#Seed@lat,lon/x,z,dist,az,pol`: the site, then the target x and z metres from it, then the distance in metres, the azimuth, and the polar angle of the camera, for example `#Auralis@-18.28,-8.91/180.0,-260.0,120.0,120.32,66.47`. The address bar follows the camera twice a second, so the link is always the view on the screen. The older forms `#Seed` and `#Seed@lat,lon` still work and take the camera a new world gets. Open a link with a site and the world lands the reader on the ground. A new seed, the dice, and a saved world always return to orbit. A gas giant never gets a probe.

On the ground the controls change, because the reader no longer turns a planet but stands on one. Moving takes the first gesture. Drag with the left button, or with one finger, to grab the ground and pull yourself over it. Hold the same press still, and you keep walking that way until you let go; steer with the pointer, because the walk follows where it points. The keys fly too, like a drone. W, A, S, and D move the probe in the flat plane, parallel to the surface: the tilt of the view does not change the plane, and nothing follows the terrain. Space or E lifts the camera, C or Q drops it, and Shift runs 3 times faster. The keys read their place on the keyboard and not their letter, so W, A, S, and D sit under the left hand on any layout. No key of the probe needs Ctrl, Cmd, or Alt, so the shortcuts of the browser and the system stay theirs. The probe keeps some momentum, so it takes about half a second to reach its speed and to stop. The side arrows turn the view, and the up and down arrows tilt it, and so do R and F. + and - zoom, so a reader with only a keyboard owns every part of the view. Every key moves the camera in a straight line and takes the target with it, so the view direction and the distance hold and only the place changes. The turn and the tilt keys turn the view about the eye, so the probe does not move. The drop stops 2 m over the terrain or over the water, and the lift stops at the ceiling. Looking takes the second gesture: drag with the right button to turn the view, and scroll to zoom. Two fingers do one thing at a time. Slide them together to look: to the side to turn, up and down to tilt, about the eye as the arrows turn it. Pinch them to zoom one to one, and twist them to turn the view as a map turns. The pan and the flight both stop at the reach. The reach limits the probe, which is the camera, and not the point the view looks at, so a turn of the view at the edge does not move the probe. The walk slows over the last 80 m instead of meeting a wall, and only the part of the step that goes outward slows, so you still walk along the edge and back in at full speed. The height of the camera sets the speeds: near the ground a wheel step moves a metre or two and a flight covers 16 m a second, at the ceiling a step moves about a hundred and a flight covers 220. The tilt is yours at every height, from a hair off straight down to 30 degrees over the horizon, so you can look up at the canopy, the clouds, and the animals that fly. The camera holds 2 m over the terrain and 500 m over the site, and it holds its height between the two: nothing pulls it over the hills, and it follows the terrain only where it lies on the floor. A click or a tap on the ground glides the view to that point in 0.8 s, and a camera more than 200 m from its target also comes a third of the way in. A glide to an animal or a plant ends between 12 and 120 m from it, so a focus always frames the thing whether you watched it from the next hill or stood beside it. A tap on an animal glides to the animal and marks it with a ring. The study chip over the dock then offers its card, so a tap always moves the view and the card never blocks the way. A tap on the ground, or Escape, takes the mark off. A drag never glides.

A landing takes the fix of the carrier on its own, and no button asks for it. The fifth block of the overlay stands at the top left of the screen: a needle that turns with the view, the bearing as three digits, the band the receiver holds, the error as an angle, and one word for the strength — faint, clear, strong, or here. A press on the block opens a short brief, and the brief follows the landing. A landing that only hears the carrier gets the brief of the distress signal: what the instrument shows, and the three landings the search takes. A landing inside two earlier wedges, or near enough to show the range, gets the next step in orbit: land on the filled cell, land a count of cells along the bearing, or cut the cross with one more wedge. A landing on the cell of the source says to follow the needle. The block pulses, with a hint that names the stage, until the reader reads the brief of that stage, and a later stage pulses again. The block is hidden on a world with no source and on a landing past the reach. The needle keeps the frame of the terrain and not the frame of the sky, so the reader walks the way it points. On the cell of the source the needle drops the globe and points at the wreck itself, and the range then falls with every step.

The Story window holds the search of the world on the screen. Each chapter has a status line: "Not heard yet", "1 fix on the globe", "3 fixes on the globe", or "Found. The log is read." Under the status line each landing of the chapter states its bearing, its error, and its cell. The Now panel and the objective state the next step. While the world has heard nothing, chapter 1 offers "Read the incident record". The record states that a carrier called for help from here and no crew came back, and that nobody knows what became of it.

The record also states that the beacon still transmits, that it reaches about a third of the way round the planet, and that a landing inside that reach catches it. So a reader who has never landed learns why the search is there. While a chapter holds a fix, "Clear the wedges" drops the wedges of that chapter and keeps the find. "How the search works" opens the brief. In orbit a found chapter offers "Find the wreck on the globe". It closes the window and turns the camera onto the cell of the wreck, and the next tap sends the probe there.

A saved world whose source the reader has found carries a mark on its thumb. Before the tune chapter 2 reads "The receiver is not tuned". After the tune it follows the ruin: "Tuned. Not heard yet", then "1 fix on the globe", "3 fixes on the globe", and "Found. The ruin is read." After both finds, chapters 1 and 2 offer "Find the wreck on the globe" and "Find the ruin on the globe", and the thumb carries two marks. The first landing after the tune pulses the block again, and its brief names an unknown signal on the band of the log.

The tune needs the frequency of the log. After the find, the card of the wreck shows a field under the last entry. The Now panel of the Story window shows the same field while the band waits for it, in orbit and on the ground. Type the number the last entry states and press Enter or Tune. A comma works as the point, the point can go (`7316` reads as `7.316`), and `MHz` after the number is optional. The receiver answers under the field: "The receiver takes a number in MHz, for example 406.025." for a text that is not a number, "The receiver holds the distress band." for `406.025`, "Locked on 7.316 MHz. The probe hears a second source." for the frequency, "A pattern under the static on 7.313 MHz." for a number within 0.050 of it, and "Static on 7.313 MHz." for any other number.

After the lock the card shows the locked band, and chapter 2 of the Story window shows the band. The carrier block takes the colour of the wedges of the ruin. While the field holds the focus, the keys type into it and the probe does not move, and `/` types a slash. Escape in the field closes the card or the window that holds it.

Stand on the cell of the source and a tap on the wreck marks it with a ring, as a tap on a plant does. The study chip then reads "Download the log" and opens the card. A tap on the ruin marks it with a ring around its disc, and the study chip reads "Study the ruin" and opens the card of the ruin. The first open of that card is the find of chapter 2.

The card of the ruin then holds the decoder of chapter 3 in the row of the way on. Click the field under the first glyph and type the name: each letter moves the focus to the next mark, a letter fills every mark of the same glyph, Backspace clears a mark and steps back, and the arrow keys move. A tap on a sound writes its letters from the field that has the focus. Enter or "Send the name" sends it, and Clear drops the letters that no send has proven. At the twin a tap on the stones marks them, and the study chip reads "Read the log of the crew". A tap on a person marks the person, and the study chip reads "Talk to" and the name.

## Natural activity

About two worlds in three have one phenomenon, never more. The world type sets the odds of each kind: a volcano with a cone, a crater, smoke and embers; a geyser that erupts every ten seconds or so; a long ragged fissure with side branches that walks across the land and stops at the shore; an aurora that folds around one pole; or a thunderstorm cell where bolts strike down from the atmosphere in two or three strokes, each with a new shape. Ice worlds get cryovolcanoes, cryogeysers, and crevasses. Gas giants get lightning in the great storm or an aurora. The Planet window shows the activity of the world.

## Fauna

Every world rolls its own species, two to four of them, from the seed. Each species is a genome:

- a class: land, air, or sub-surface, chosen to fit a niche (meadow, forest, beach, lowland, dune, snow, ash, open sea, cloud deck);
- a locomotion: monopod, biped, tripod, quadruped, hexapod, serpent; gas sac, wings, fins; or a breathing arch, a periscope neck, or a plough that swims under the ground;
- a body plan: blob, spindle, chain, dome, disc, or a swarm of shards around a core;
- a head: beak, mandibles, eye stalks, lure, crest, tusks, or none;
- extras: sail, spikes, lamp beads, tendrils, garden, plates, tail, flukes, antennae, mounds;
- a sociality: solitary, a pair, or a herd of four to fourteen, with the size of the formation in metres.

The geometry is built from those parts. Every vertex carries a rig record (mode, phase, amplitude, weight) and a pivot, and one shader animates all species: legs swing about the hip and fold at the knee on the forward stroke, wing sheets roll at the root and bend toward the tip, tendrils and tails sway, lures and beads pulse, heads nod, flukes lag the body. A per-species carriage moves the whole body: a walk bob, a hop, a wave, a float, an arch pulse, or a rise and sink. Legs only swing while the animal actually moves.

The lore is modular too. The name, the binomial, the habitat, the size, the diet, the manner, and the story are all assembled from the parts the animal really has, so a lantern-headed tripod waits in ambush and a plated hexapod licks minerals from the rock. The manner and the story also say how the animal groups: a grazer moves in herds of twelve, a jawed strider hunts in pairs, and a serpent keeps to itself. The size text gives the real size of the animal in metres, and the ground scale uses the same number.

The lore also reads the planet. `lore.js` turns the temperature, the gravity, the day length, the moon count, the rings, the ocean, the plants, and the one natural activity into a set of short tags, and every line of text names the tags it needs and the tags it forbids. A line about rain only reaches a world with liquid water and a sea. A line about a tide needs a moon to raise it and a sea that is not frozen. A line that says "bark" needs a world with trees. A line that says "alone" never reaches a herd animal. A line gated on a condition outranks a line that would fit any planet, so two worlds with the same creature read differently: the same hopper explains its legs by the gravity on one planet and by the length of the night on the next.

The species of one world also know about each other. After every animal has a name, the engine walks the pairs and rolls a relation: one hunts another, one rides the back of another and cleans it, one works the mound the burrower pushes up, one flies in the lee of the sky whale. A relation writes a sentence into both stories, so the two agree.

`tools/lore-audit/audit.mjs` reviews the permutations without a browser. It sweeps every world type against every value it can reach — 11,616 worlds — times every class, every sociality, every locomotion, every head, and every part. It checks that no text slot is ever empty and that no line is unreachable; that no line claims a fact the world does not have; that an animal is never offered two sources of food; and that every relation rule, run against every pair of creature shapes, keeps to the contract its sentence implies, so a blind burrower is never described gathering at a light. Run `node tools/lore-audit/audit.mjs --seeds 200` to add two hundred real worlds through the worker.

### The fauna lab

`tools/fauna-lab.html` shows creatures without a world roll. It builds genomes with the real `buildCreature()` and `faunaMaterial()` from `fauna.js`, and it draws each one in its own animated cell with its triangle count. Serve the directory and open <http://localhost:5555/tools/fauna-lab.html>.

The page has a form on the left and a grid on the right:

- **Creature**: the locomotion, the body plan, the head, the extras, the wing form, and the whale head. Point to a value to see what it is.
- **Numbers**: the body radius, the legs, the segments, and the rates of the animation. An empty field takes the middle of the range that `species.js` draws from.
- **Compare**: the `Rows` and `Columns` fields each select one value to change across the grid. Set both to "—" to see one creature in one large cell.
- **View**: the colours, the detail (`coarse` is the far body of the ground), the camera, and a time that stops the clock.

The lists come from `Species.CATALOGUE` in `species.js`, so a new locomotion, plan, head, or extra shows in the lab with no edit to the page. A cell of one of the ten forms (the ripple, the garland, the hoop, the mantle, the parasol, the choir, the scree, the islet, the listener, and the wake) carries the name of the form; see "The forms" in `docs/fauna.md`. When "Only forms a world can roll" is set, a dashed cell shows a combination that no world can make. Clear it to build every combination. Click a cell to copy its creature to the form. A gold border marks the creature of the form.

Each field that is not at its default is a URL parameter, so the address shares the view. For example, `tools/fauna-lab.html?loco=wings&rows=wingStyle&cols=extra&t=1.3` compares the three wing forms with each extra. Headless Chrome can save a still for a review:

```sh
"/Applications/Google Chrome.app/Contents/MacOS/Google Chrome" --headless=new --use-angle=swiftshader --enable-unsafe-swiftshader --window-size=1300,900 --virtual-time-budget=5000 --screenshot=wings.png "http://localhost:5555/tools/fauna-lab.html?loco=wings&rows=wingStyle&cols=extra&t=1.3"
```

A browser can keep an old `fauna.js` in its module cache after an edit. Do a hard reload before you trust a triangle count.

See `docs/fauna.md` for the architecture: the genome fields, the rig modes, the carriages, and the placement rules.

Creatures roam on procedural paths. Two slow oscillators with per-creature random frequencies steer each one, a leash pulls it back toward its home spot, and grazers stop and start on a third oscillator. Land creatures follow a coarse height map from the worker and turn back at the shoreline. Zoom in close and click a creature to open the inspector: a live turntable with the path trace and the species backstory. That first open adds the creature to the field guide, and its card in the Planet window then opens the inspector as well.

On the ground the same creatures walk in groups at their real size. One mover carries the group, and the members hold a formation around it, so a herd of nine crosses a hill as one body and a solitary animal roams alone. The size text of the species is the size of the animal against the terrain: a quad of "4.5 m at the shoulder" stands 4.5 m tall over a grid of 2 m squares. A creature close to the reader is the full body, and one at distance is a coarse body of under 80 triangles that the same shader animates. See the "Ground tier" section of `docs/fauna.md`.

## Flora

The plants of a patch carry lore too, and it is written for the patch and not for the world. An animal belongs to a planet, so its text is written once, when the world is generated. A plant belongs to the ground it stands on: the patch decides which of the sixteen kinds grow there, how many of each, and how tall they stand, and the biome under them is a fact of the site. So the worker writes the plant lore inside the patch build, and the text rides back with the ground.

Every line of text reads three tag sources at once: the planet, the kind, and the biome. A line about a thaw needs a ground that freezes. A line about gills needs a fungus. A line about roots under water needs a shore or a bed, and a forest floor only earns a line about ground that holds water. The planet's own flora tags are stripped out first, because a desert world carries the word "cactus" for what its globe grows somewhere, and a card has to say what the plant in front of the reader is.

The flora also reads the temperature of the site rather than the mean of the planet. The stats card states the mean; a patch high on a mountain is colder, and a snow field is at or below freezing whatever the planet averages.

The numbers on the card are the numbers on the ground. The height row states the median of the kind and the tallest one on this patch, and the stand line states the count, all measured off the plants the patch actually placed. A reader can walk out and check them.

The plants of one patch know each other. After every kind has a name, the engine walks the pairs and rolls a relation: a tall kind shades a low one, a colossus holds a court of smaller plants around its roots, a fungus takes the dead wood of a tree, a whip runs up anything tall enough to carry it. A relation writes a sentence into both stories. One more line reaches across to the fauna and names an animal of the world — what browses it, what shelters under it, what carries its spores, what walks around its spines.

The controls are the ones the animals use, and they only work while the probe is down. A tap on a plant marks it: a ring lies on the ground around it, and the study chip over the dock offers "Study this plant". After the find the chip reads "Study the ‹name›". The card holds the habitat, the height, the food, and the way it spreads. The card has no arrows, because an arrow would step to a plant the reader has not found yet. On the ground the field guide of the Planet window lists the plants of the patch, and a plant the reader has not studied shows as a locked card.

The preview on the card turns the plant instead of the camera. An animal walks, so its card lets it roam and follows it. A plant stands still, so the card holds the eye and rotates the body on its own axis, one turn in about twenty-four seconds. The plant still sways in the wind and breathes on the card, because it is built with the same material the ground gives it.

`tools/lore-audit/audit.mjs` sweeps the flora as well as the fauna: every sky a flora gate can tell apart, times sixteen kinds, times ten biomes. It checks that no slot is ever empty, that no line is unreachable, that no word claims a fact the site does not have, that no plant is offered two sources of food, and that no relation reaches a pair it does not suit. `tools/lore-audit/patch-sample.mjs` prints whole stories from real patches.

See `docs/flora.md` for the architecture.
